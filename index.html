AortaFlow

A latent-ODE model that learns how blood actually moves through the stenotic aorta, not just how fast it peaks.

  • Two distinct flow components found across 159 patients
  • Manuscript in preparation

Yufan (Viola) Wu, Yusuf A. Ozturk, Fanwei Kong, Jessica Wagenseil
Washington University in St. Louis

One number decides the diagnosis

Aortic stenosis is common in older adults. Between 2 and 9 percent of Americans over 65 are diagnosed with it.

The diagnosis rests mainly on one measurement: peak velocity, the fastest speed blood reaches as it leaves the valve.

But patients who share the same peak velocity do not share the same flow. Blood can hug the wall or stay near the center. It can peak early or late. It can stay organized or swirl.

These differences matter. Disturbed flow acts on the aortic wall over years, and is linked to dissection and thrombosis.

AortaFlow learns the whole flow field instead of one number, and then asks whether patients naturally fall into different flow types.

How it works

The input is the full 3D velocity field through the aorta over one cardiac cycle, for 159 patients.

That is far too much data to model point by point, so the pipeline has four steps.

  • Compress each snapshot.Proper orthogonal decomposition (POD) reduces each instant of the 3D flow field to 15 numbers, keeping the dominant flow structures.
  • Read the history.An ODE-RNN encoder reads the sequence of 50 snapshots and infers a single starting state for that patient.
  • Evolve it in continuous time.A latent ODE moves that state forward. Because it is a differential equation, flow can be evaluated at any time point, not only at the sampled frames.
  • Decode back to flow.An MLP decoder turns the state at any time into POD coefficients, which expand back into a full 3D velocity field.

At inference, the starting state is refined for each new patient with a short gradient optimization, so the model fits an unseen case without retraining.

It reproduces the flow faithfully

Predicted flow tracks the ground truth closely across the cardiac cycle, with R² = 0.85 for velocity.

The same model also recovers pressure with R² = 0.87, even though pressure was not the focus. It outperformed several baselines.

Two flow components emerge

Each patient's flow becomes a single trajectory in the learned space. When we cluster those trajectories, the cohort splits cleanly in two.

Nobody labeled these groups. They came out of the flow itself.

Decoding each component back into a velocity field shows what separates them.

They differ in more than speed

Eight flow features separate the two groups, and speed is only one of them.

Component 1 is the slower group. It has a lower peak velocity, a lower mean velocity, and a gentler slowdown after the peak.

But on every other measure, component 1 is the higher one. Its peak comes later in the cycle, sits further from the center of the lumen, arrives at a larger angle, is more concentrated in time, and is accompanied by greater flow displacement in the ascending aorta.

This is the important part. The slower group is not simply the milder group. Its flow peaks further from the center and is more displaced in the ascending aorta, and those are exactly the patterns linked to long-term wall damage.

A peak-velocity reading alone would put these two groups in the same box.

Why this matters

AortaFlow turns a full 3D, time-varying flow field into a small state that a computer can carry around, compare, and evolve forward.

  • Flow on demand, without a new simulation.Once trained, the model produces the velocity field at any point in the cardiac cycle in a fraction of the time a full CFD run takes.
  • A flow type, not just a number.Flow type is a richer descriptor than peak velocity, and it can be assigned automatically. It gives a clinician a second axis of information.
  • Continuous time by construction.Because the core is a differential equation, the model handles irregular sampling and can interpolate between frames. That fits real imaging data, which is rarely evenly spaced.
  • A building block for a digital twin.Pair the flow model with a shape model and you have both the geometry and the hemodynamics of a patient in one compact representation.

These results are from a single cohort and no clinical outcomes are attached yet. Linking component to long-term outcome is the next step.

Technical details
  • Data. Stabilized aortic flow for 159 patients, 50 snapshots per cardiac cycle, velocity in cylindrical components (vs, vr, vθ) along the vessel centerline.
  • Reduction. POD projection of the mean-subtracted field onto 15 modes per snapshot.
  • Model. Variational autoencoder with a latent ODE core: ODE-RNN encoder with GRU updates infers q(z₀), a neural ODE evolves the latent state, and an MLP decoder returns POD coefficients.
  • Inference. The initial state z₀ is refined per patient with 500 Adam steps.
  • Grouping. Functional PCA of the latent trajectories, then a spherical Gaussian mixture with k = 2 over 15 dimensions.
  • Accuracy. R² = 0.85 for velocity coefficients, R² = 0.87 for pressure coefficients; outperforms several sequence baselines.
